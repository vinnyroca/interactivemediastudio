# New Engine

## Description

For this project, we will be exploring how artists, animators, and indie game designers are using the technology of game engines to craft new and exciting interactive media artworks. While previous units have explore the historical relationship between media technologies and interactive media art, this unit explores current developments in game engine technologies.

In line with the themes of this unit, for this project, each student will either develop a new work of interactive media art that explores one of the follow topics, or can continue developing the first or second project developed within the course:

Topics to explore include:

- Game Engine Cinema
- Simulation
- Zero Player Games
- Social Critique Games
- Exploration Games
- Narrative Games
- Queer Games
- Remix Games
- Mechanics Forward Games
- Toy-like Games

## Close Play and Journal

In addition to assigned readings and media, students must complete a close play of two games during this unit. Students should play each of their chosen game for 1 hour each. Critically playing for a longer period of time is strongly encouraged. It is recommended that students browse through the list of games and look up a trailer or long play of each game before making a decision on what games to play. Students are also encourage to play more than two games on the list.

Student should approach their play using a close play methodology outlined in this [article](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/j/jcms/18261332.0064.905/--close-playing?rgn=main;view=fulltext) by Edmond Y. Chang.

As part of the close play assignment for this project, you will also be submitting a game journal. Your game journal should be around 200 words per game and should informally respond to the games based on the topics and ideas we are covering in class.

You may find the following prompts helpful as you write each journal entry (From _The Well-Read Game: On Playing Thoughtfully_, Fullerton & Farber, 2025)

- Describe your experiences in the game as if you are describing a dream or travelling to a new place. (Use the first person, present tense.)
- How does the game begin — Where are you? Who are you playing? What do you want or need to do? As the game unfolds, what happens that moves you? Do you relate to any characters or situations? What do you bring to the game from your own lived experiences?
- Are there peak moments of play that make you want to capture them as screenshots or to remember

Students should either choose 2 games/video from their chosen topic, or, if they are developing a previous project, should consult with the instructor to choose two games that will assist in furthering their project.

A list of games/videos can be found here: [New Engines](/newenginelecture/)

## Proposal

 If you are continuing to develop a previous project, write a brief reflection on where you are in the project right now, and where you plan to take the project.

If you are developing a new project, write a brief project proposal. Within your proposal, answer the following questions. Bring a printed copy of your proposal to class to discuss with the instructor.

1. What is the working title of your project?
2. Which topic are you planning to explore with your project.
3. What are the art assets that will make up your project?
4. How will you produce these assets?
5. What is your plan for programming the project? Will you need to learn additional skills? What is your timeline between now and the due date of the project?
6. What additional games or other forms of media will you research and explore to produce this project?

### Rubric

**1:** The student answered all questions and clearly articulated the design intentions of their project.

**0-1:** The student answered some to all questions and mostly articulated the design intentions of their project.

## Prototype

Submit a prototype that includes all the major interactions your project will include. Your project does not need to include your final art assets. This should be a gray box prototype; meaning your project should include your most difficult to program systems and not your final art assets. As the point of your prototype is to get feedback on your in-progress project, you should have enough of your project complete to be able to get peer and instructor feedback. Your project must be playable on the due date of the prototype. Students should prepare questions for peer and instructor feedback on their prototype.

**2:** The student's prototype is playable and conveys the main ideas of the project. Peers and the instructor are able to provide feedback based on the level of completeness of the prototype. The student is prepared with questions to ask about their project.

**1:** The student's prototype is playable, however, the project is completed to such a degree that it is difficult to understand the student's intentions. As a result, peers and the instructor may have difficulty providing feedback on the project.

**0:** The student's prototype is non-functional or is incomplete

### Submit

Bring your playable prototype to class and have it set up for students and the instructor to engage with.

### Concept (7 points)

**6-7:** The student went above expectations in their conceptual approach to the project. The student approached the concept for the project with creative/intellectual generosity and was able to demonstrate conceptual risk-taking in their project.

**4-5**: The student met expectation in their conceptual approach to the project. The student presented a thoughtful interpretation of the project prompt.

**0-3:** The student did not meet expectation in their conceptual approach to the project. The student did not demonstrate an understanding of the assignment. The assignment might be missing required elements or shows little effort to consider the project prompt.

### Code (7 points)

**6-7:** Within the project, the student used their programming knowledge to create a conceptually and technically complex system. The student made use of materials learned in class as well as explored new approaches to using Unity. The student is able to clearly communicate their system and the programming concepts they used to design their project. The program is fully functional.

**4-5:** Within the project the student used their programming knowledge to create a functional system. The student only made use of functions learned in class. The student can communicate their system and the programming concepts they used to design their project. The program contains some bugs but is functional.

**0-3:** The student project is either non-functional or barely functional. While effort was made to create the project, the final code contains bugs or errors that causes the program to be unable to run. The student did not seek help from the instructor or peers in advance of submission. The program may not be functional.

### Craft (7 points)

**6-7:** The student demonstrated exceptional thoughtfulness and intentionality in their visual, text, audio, and interactive design. Visual, text, audio, and interactive elements of the project are clearly connected with the student conceptual approach.

**4-5:** The visual, text, audio, and interactive elements of the project is somewhat intentional and can be connected to the conceptual approach to the project.

**0-3:** The visual, text, and interactive elements of the project are unrelated to the conceptual approach to the project.

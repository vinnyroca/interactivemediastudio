# Arcades Everywhere

## Description

For this project, your aim is to create a work of interactive media art that is designed to be played in public. This project may take many forms, from a work that may embarrass players/onlookers, a work that will inspire cheer and applause, and further. 

Your project should be designed for a particular public. Perhaps it is designed to be played by a campus group you are involved with. Perhaps it is designed for a particular location. For whom are you designing? Additionally, you should consider the values you project is communicated through both its design and mechanics.

Considering the games discussed during this unit, your project should be simple to understand, easy to begin playing, and if you choose, difficult to advance after a certain point. Of course, these parameters can be tweaked and altered depending on the publics you have in mind. Your final submission will include a work of interactive media that will be played and critiqued and class, as well as a piece of writing that situates your work within the class readings.
## Framing

While it might seem that games played in public disappeared with the Arcade, public forms of gameplay still exist within contemporary culture.  

### Mobile

Perhaps public play just became synonymous with smart phones; an act of public computing many of us constantly experience. If you own an iPhone you might even note that Apple's game subscription service is called Apple Arcade. People are playing games everywhere: in parks, on trains, in class, or waiting in line. While these mobile games might not look visually similar to the games of early arcades (See: [Love in Deep Space](https://loveanddeepspace.infoldgames.com/en-EN/home)), many of these games include similar tactics of getting players into a [flow state](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262045506/against-flow/) to continually extract value. While not true of all mobile games, many of these games focus on a small definable loop that can be played endlessly for high scores (See Candy Crush Saga, Subway Surfers, Fruit Ninja, Clash of Clans, Flappy Bird). At the same time, artists like [Zach Gage](http://www.stfj.net/) are exploring the limits of the casual game space as an attempt to see if interesting work can be made when we focus on quick yet complex interactions.

### Galleries

Games in public can now also be seen widely within art galleries. From the works of [Danielle Brathwaite-Shirley](https://buffaloakg.org/person/danielle-brathwaite-shirley), [Alex Mackin Dolan](https://www.alexmackindolan.com/brain-nourishment/), to [Lawrence Lek](https://hammer.ucla.edu/exhibitions/2025/lawrence-lek-nox-high-rise), artists are exploring the aesthetics of games and interactive media to think through a variety of cultural, political and economic ideas.

### New Arcades

Aside from art galleries, independent game makers are still continuing to produce arcades and arcade cabinets. Oftentimes less focused on conceptual and aesthetic questions and more focused on game play, these arcade games are continuing to push the bounds of public play. A variety of these arcade games can be found at independent arcade venues such as [Wonderville](https://www.wonderville.nyc/) in NYC. One notable public independent project is the arcade cabinet [Nave](https://videogamo.com/navearcade/?lang=eng) by Videogamo, a traveling arcade cabinet that maintains high sorces across locations. Notably academic explorations of public game play include [UCLA's Game Art Festival](https://festival.games.ucla.edu/) and NYU's [No Quarter](https://gamecenter.nyu.edu/events/no-quarter/) Exhibitions.

![members of videgamo standing with nave arcade cabinet in middle of road](/Attachments/Pasted%20image%2020260831115957.png)

Nave arcade Cabinet by Videogamo

>"NAVE is a traveling videogame. What exactly is that? In 2010, we started to program NAVE as a computer game, but then we realized that the game was meant to be played on a videogame arcade machine, like those from the 70's, 80's and 90's."


### Streaming

Additionally, as T. L. Taylor notes in her book [Watch Me Play: Twitch and the Rise of Game Live Streaming](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691183558/watch-me-play), games are being played in online publics for millions of viewers. Many game developers also note having this form of public in mind when making a game. A common adage in game development is that games that get picked up by streamers sell. We see this effect with games such as [Goat Simulator](https://store.steampowered.com/app/265930/Goat_Simulator/), [Getting Over It with Bennet Foddy](https://store.steampowered.com/app/240720/Getting_Over_It_with_Bennett_Foddy/), [Among Us](https://store.steampowered.com/app/945360/Among_Us/), [Phasmophobia](https://store.steampowered.com/app/739630/Phasmophobia/), [Lethal Company](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1966720/Lethal_Company/), and [Meccha Chameleon](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4704690/MECCHA_CHAMELEON/). Consider what about these games makes them compelling for both audiences to watch and streamers to play.

## Close Play and Journal

In addition to assigned readings and media, students must complete a close play of two games during this unit. Students should play each of their chosen game for 1 hour each. Critically playing for a longer period of time is strongly encouraged. It is recommended that students browse through the list of games and look up a trailer or long play of each game before making a decision on what games to play. Students are also encourage to play more than two games on the list.

Student should approach their play using a close play methodology outlined in this [article](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/j/jcms/18261332.0064.905/--close-playing?rgn=main;view=fulltext) by Edmond Y. Chang.

The below selection of games is a curated list of contemporary independent video games that embody many of the design principles of arcade games or games played in public. All of these games are relatively easy to learn, can be picked up at anytime, for any duration, and are difficult to become experts in. Some of these games such as *Hades*, *Neon White* and *Celeste* are connected to a larger story, however, they still contain an addictive and twitchy game loop that keeps them hooked. These games were chosen not necessarily because they are played in public, but because most of them contain simple and refined game loops that work well in public or streamed settings.

All of these games are either available through Steam or Itch on the class computers. While students may play these games on their own devices, it is encourage that the class finds a shared time to meet within the lab to play these games together.

As part of the close play assignment for this project, you will also be submitting a game journal. Your game journal should be around 200 words per game and should informally respond to the games based on the topics and ideas we are covering in class. 

You may find the following prompts helpful as you write each journal entry (From _The Well-Read Game: On Playing Thoughtfully_, Fullerton & Farber, 2025)

- Describe your experiences in the game as if you are describing a dream or travel to a new place. (Use the first person, present tense.)

- How does the game begin — Where are you? Who are you playing? What do you want or need to do? As the game unfolds, what happens that moves you? Do you relate to any characters or situations? What do you bring to the game from your own lived experiences?

- Are there peak moments of play that make you want to capture them as screenshots or to remember

## Proposal

Within your proposal answer the following questions. Bring a printed copy of your proposal to class to discuss with the instructor.

1. What is the working title of your project?
2. In one paragraph, describe the experience of your project.
3. If there is a goal, What is the goal of your interactive project?
4. What are the audience’s actions within your project?
5. What is the concept of your project and how will it relate to the interactions?
6. How do you imagine the project will make audiences feel?
7. What public are you designing your project for?
8. What is your plan for programming the project? Will you need to learn additional skills? What is your timeline between now and the due date of the project?

Your project must be playable on the due date of the prototype. Students should prepare questions for peer and instructor feedback on their prototype.

**2:** The student's prototype is playable and conveys the main ideas of the project. Peers and the instructor are able to provide feedback based on the level of completeness of the prototype. The student is prepare with questions to ask about their project.

**4-5:** Within the project, the student used their programming knowledge to to create a conceptually and technically complex system. The student made use of materials learned in class as well as explored new approaches to using Unity. The student is able to clearly communicate their system and the programming concepts they used to design their project. The program is full functional.

**0-2:** The visual, text, and interactive elements of the project are unrelated to the conceptual approach to the project.

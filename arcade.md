# Arcades Everywhere

## Description

While it might seems that games played in public disappeared with the Arcade, public forms of gameplay still exist within contemporary culture.  

Perhaps public play just became synonymous with smart phones; an act of public computing many of us constantly experience. If you own an iPhone you might even note that Apple's game subscription service is called Apple Arcade. People are playing games everywhere: in parks, on trains, in class, or waiting in line. While these mobile games might not look visually similar to the games of early arcades (See: [Love in Deep Space](https://loveanddeepspace.infoldgames.com/en-EN/home)), many of these games include similar tactics of getting players into a [flow state](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262045506/against-flow/) to continually extract value in the form of payment. While not true of all mobile games, many of these games focus on a small definable loop that can be played endlessly for high scores (See Candy Crush Saga, Subway Surfers, Fruit Ninga, Clash of Clans, Flappy Bird). At the same time, artists like [Zach Gage](http://www.stfj.net/) are exploring the limits of the casual game space as attempts to see if interesting work can be made when we focus on quick yet complex interactions.

Games in public can now also be seen widely within art galleries. From the works of [Danielle Brathwaite-Shirley](https://buffaloakg.org/person/danielle-brathwaite-shirley), [Alex Mackin Dolan](https://www.alexmackindolan.com/brain-nourishment/), to [Lawrence Lek](https://hammer.ucla.edu/exhibitions/2025/lawrence-lek-nox-high-rise), artists are exploring the aesthetics of games and interactive media to think through a variety of cultural, political and economic ideas. Aside from art galleries, independent game makers are still continuing to produce arcades and arcade cabinets. Oftentimes less focused on conceptual and aesthetic questions and more focused on game play, these arcade games are continuing to push the bounds of public play. A variety of these arcade games can be found at independent arcade venues such as [Wonderville](https://www.wonderville.nyc/) in NYC. One notable public independent public project is the arcade cabinet [Nave](https://videogamo.com/navearcade/?lang=eng) by Videogamo, a traveling arcade cabinet that maintains highsorces across locations. Notably academic explorations of public game play include [UCLA's Game Art Festival](https://festival.games.ucla.edu/) and NYU's [No Quarter](https://gamecenter.nyu.edu/events/no-quarter/) Exhibitions.

Additionally, as T. L. Taylor notes in her book [Watch Me Play: Twitch and the Rise of Game Live Streaming](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691183558/watch-me-play), games are being played in online publics for millions of viewers. Many game developers also note have this form of public in mind when making a game. The short hand is that games that get picked up by streamers sell. We see this effect with games such as [Goat Simulator](https://store.steampowered.com/app/265930/Goat_Simulator/), [Getting Over It with Bennet Foddy](https://store.steampowered.com/app/240720/Getting_Over_It_with_Bennett_Foddy/), [Among Us](https://store.steampowered.com/app/945360/Among_Us/), [Phasmophobia](https://store.steampowered.com/app/739630/Phasmophobia/), [Lethal Company](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1966720/Lethal_Company/), and [Meccha Chameleon](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4704690/MECCHA_CHAMELEON/). Consider what about these games makes them compelling for both audiences to watch and streamers to play.

For this project, your aim is to create a work of interactive media art that is designed to be played in public. This project may take many forms, from a work that may embarrass players/onlookers, a work that will inspire cheer and applause, and further. Considering, the games discussed during this unit, your project should be simple to understand, easy to begin playing, and if you choose, difficult to advance after a certain point. Of course, these parameters can be tweaked and altered depending on the publics you have in mind. Your final submission will include and work of interactive media that will be played and critiqued and class, as well as a piece of writing that situates your work within the class readings.

## Close Play and Journal

In addition to assigned readings and media, student must complete a close play of two games during this unit. Students should play each of their chosen game for 1 hour each. Critically playing for a longer period of time is strongly encouraged. It is recommended that students browse through the list of games and look up a trailer or long play of each game before making their decisions. Students are also encourage more than two games on the list.

Student should approach their play using a close play methodology outlined in this [article](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/j/jcms/18261332.0064.905/--close-playing?rgn=main;view=fulltext) by Edmond Y. Chang.

The below selection of games is a curated list of contemporary independent video games that embody many of the design principles of arcade games or games played in public. All of these games are relatively easy to learn, can be picked up at anytime, for any duration, and are difficult to become experts in. Some of these games such as Hades, Neon White and Celeste are connected to a larger story, however, they still contain an addictive and twitchy game loops that keeps them hooked. These games were chosen not necessarily because they are played in public, but because most of them contain simple and refined game loops that work well in public or streamed settings.

All of these game are either available through Steam or Itch on the class computers. While students may play these games on their own devices, it is encourage that the class finds a shared time to meet within the lab to play these games together.

As part of the close play assignment for this project, you will also be submitting a game journal. Your game journal should be around 200 words per game and should informally respond to the games based on the topics and ideas we are covering in class. 

You may find the following prompts helpful as you write each journal entry (From _The Well-Read Game: On Playing Thoughtfully_, Fullerton & Farber, 2025)

- Describe your experiences in the game as if you are describing a dream or travel to a new place. (Use the first person, present tense.)

- How does the game begin — Where are you? Who are you playing? What do you want or need to do? As the game unfolds, what happens that moves you? Do you relate to any characters or situations? What do you bring to the game from your own lived experiences?

- Are there peak moments of play that make you want to capture them as screenshots or to remember

## Proposal

Within your proposal answer the following questions. Bring a printed copy of your proposal to class to discuss with the instructor.

1. What is the working title of your project?
2. In one paragraph, describe the experience of your game.
3. If there is a goal, What is the goal of your interactive project?
4. What are the audience’s actions within your project?
5. What is the concept of your project and how will it relate to the interactions?
6. How do you imagine the project will make audiences feel?
7. What public are you designing you project for?
8. What is your plan for programming the project? Will you need to learn additional skills? What is your timeline between now and the due date of the project?

### Rubric

**0-1:** The student answered some to all questions and mostly articulated the design intentions of their project.
